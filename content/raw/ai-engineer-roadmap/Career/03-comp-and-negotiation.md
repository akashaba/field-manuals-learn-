# Career 03 — Compensation & Negotiation for Remote AI Roles

> **Goal:** Walk into offer conversations knowing what a Senior AI Engineer role should pay in 2026, the components of a comp package, and how to negotiate without leaving money on the table or blowing up the offer. Specific attention to remote / US-vs-EU / cross-border considerations.

---

## The 2026 Landscape (Rough Ranges)

Comp is complicated. It moves quickly. What follows is an honest overview of the market shape as of late 2026; verify current numbers via Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, and your recruiter's own data.

**Total comp (TC) means base + bonus + equity/year for public co's; base + equity/year for startups.**

### Senior Software Engineer / Senior AI Engineer, US remote

| Company tier | Base | Bonus | Equity/yr | TC range |
|-------------|------|-------|-----------|----------|
| **FAANG-adjacent (Meta, Google, Amazon, Apple, Netflix)** | $180-220k | 10-20% | $200-400k | $400-650k |
| **AI natives (Anthropic, OpenAI, Mistral, xAI)** | $200-260k | 10-20% | $250-500k+ | $500-800k+ |
| **Well-funded startups (Series B-D, ~$50-500M raised)** | $150-200k | 0-10% | $50-200k (liquid val TBD) | $200-400k paper |
| **Mid-size tech (Stripe, Datadog, Airbnb, etc.)** | $170-210k | 10-20% | $100-250k | $300-500k |
| **Enterprise / traditional tech (Oracle, IBM, Salesforce)** | $150-190k | 10-15% | $50-150k | $200-350k |
| **Early startup (seed, Series A)** | $130-170k | 0-5% | Larger % of a small pie | $150-250k liquid; upside speculative |
| **Consulting shops (McKinsey Tech, BCG X)** | $150-200k | 20-40% | Small/none | $200-350k |
| **Government / non-profit** | $110-160k | 0 | 0 | $110-160k |

Staff / Principal levels add 30-100% on top.

### EU / UK / Remote European roles

Numbers roughly 40-70% of US equivalent, sometimes closer for elite AI firms:

| Region | Senior SWE/AI TC (approximate) |
|--------|-------------------------------|
| **London (UK)** | £110-180k base + equity (public), Anthropic/OAI London much higher |
| **Berlin / Amsterdam / Zurich** | €90-150k base for most companies; €150-250k+ for elite AI firms |
| **Paris** | €80-130k typical; €120-200k+ at elite firms |
| **Warsaw / Lisbon / Barcelona** | €60-110k typical (cost of living lower) |
| **Full-remote European** | Wide range; often set by employer's HQ region + local adjustments |

For Brian specifically (US-based, targeting remote US or European): US roles will pay more. European roles from a US employer often use the US pay bands adjusted by cost-of-living to your Helena location, which is favorable. European roles from a European employer typically use their local band regardless of where you live.

### Location adjustments (US employers, remote worker)

Some US companies use "geo-tiered" comp; some pay a single national band; some pay SF Bay Area rates everywhere.

- **Tier 1 (SF Bay, NYC, Seattle)**: highest bands (multiplier ~1.0)
- **Tier 2 (LA, Chicago, DC, Boston)**: 0.90-0.95 of Tier 1
- **Tier 3 (Denver, Austin, Portland, Miami)**: 0.85-0.90
- **Tier 4 (rest of US including Helena)**: 0.75-0.90

Some companies (Netflix, Airbnb, Anthropic historically) don't do geo-tiering — one national band. Prefer those if geographic flexibility matters to you.

## The Components of an Offer

Every offer letter has these components. Understand each before negotiating.

**Base salary.** The reliable cash. Paid biweekly or monthly. Typically 40-70% of TC. **Increases here compound** — future raises are % of base.

**Sign-on bonus.** One-time cash at start (or split over year 1). $20k-$100k+ at senior level. Often subject to clawback if you leave within 1-2 years. **Negotiable in almost all offers.**

**Annual performance bonus.** % of base, paid annually, target-based. Common at big cos (10-25%). Startups often have no annual bonus.

**Equity — RSUs (public companies)** — Restricted Stock Units. Grant is denominated in $ (say $200k over 4 years, so ~$50k/year), vesting typically 25% per year (or 15/25/25/25/10 style). Value tied to stock price at vest, not grant. Public co stocks are liquid; you can sell on vest (subject to blackout / lockup).

**Equity — ISOs / NSOs (startups)** — options to buy shares at a fixed strike price. Vest schedule same. Value = current price - strike, but only realized when you sell. Requires an IPO or acquisition. **Speculative. Value stated in offer letters is usually optimistic.**

**Equity — Anthropic-style tender opportunities.** Some private companies do periodic tender offers where employees can sell some of their vested equity to secondary investors. This is *huge* for an otherwise-illiquid asset. Ask if it applies.

**Benefits.** Health / dental / vision (US-important); 401k match; parental leave; PTO; wellness budget; home office stipend; laptop budget. Usually inflexible; occasionally negotiable at senior levels.

**Cliff.** Most equity has a 1-year cliff — leave before 1 year, get zero equity. After the cliff, vesting accelerates.

**Refresh grants.** Additional equity granted at each performance review, on top of the initial grant. At public tech co's a "refresh" is often 25-50% of initial grant per year. Ask about the refresh policy.

**Level.** The single most impactful thing. L5 vs L6 at Google (or E6 vs E7 at Meta) is a $150-300k/year TC difference. **Negotiate level, not just comp.**

## Doing the Research

Before any offer conversation:

1. **Levels.fyi** — https://levels.fyi. Best resource for real self-reported comp data at named companies and levels. Filter by remote, level, and year (fresher data > older).
2. **Glassdoor** — less accurate but useful for benefits and interview reports.
3. **Blind** (blind.com) — anonymous employee posts. Useful for culture and comp intel at specific cos; caveats for both.
4. **Your recruiter** — will share company bands if asked directly. Just say: "Can you tell me the range for this level in my location?"
5. **Your network** — 2-3 friends at the target company or a peer company. One dinner conversation is worth 20 online searches. Ask specifically: "if you were negotiating today, what would you push for?"
6. **Peer AI engineer LinkedIn** — check what similar-background people are getting.

Build a comp target sheet for each company you're interviewing at:

```
Company: Acme AI
Role: Senior AI Engineer
Level: L5 / IC5
Location: US remote (Helena)
Data sources: 3 Levels.fyi data points 2025-2026, 1 friend inside

Base: $180-200k (target $190k)
Sign-on: $30-60k (target $40k, willing to accept $20k)
Equity: $200-350k over 4yr (target $280k)
Total: $325-450k (target $370k, walk-away $290k)

Key negotiable levers: level (push for L5 not L4), refresh %, sign-on
Non-negotiable: base < $170k, level < L5
```

Have this **before** the offer arrives. Winging it live is where money is left on the table.

## The Actual Negotiation

The offer conversation is a specific dance. Rules:

### Rule 1 — Never accept on the phone.

Recruiter says: "I have an offer for you — $170k base, $30k sign-on, $180k RSUs over 4 years." Total: $237.5k/yr.

Wrong response: "Wow, thank you, I accept!"

Right response: "That's exciting to hear. Thank you. Can you send me the details in writing? I'll review and get back to you in the next 2-3 days."

**Always take 24-72 hours minimum before responding.** Signals discipline, prevents impulse, gives you time to compare and calculate.

### Rule 2 — Anchor high, negotiate from the top.

If the offer is $237.5k TC and you know the band goes to $300k, your counter targets the top of the band, not the middle. You will meet in the middle of *that* range — so anchoring higher pulls the settling point up.

Counter template:
> "Thanks for the offer — I'm excited about the role. I've done some research and calibrated with a couple of other conversations I'm currently in. Based on the level and my background, I was hoping for a package closer to $290k total — I could see the mix being $210k base, $50k sign-on, and $240k RSUs. Is there flexibility to move up on any of these?"

Notice:
- Positive framing ("excited about the role") — this is a collaboration, not a fight
- Cite research + market ("done some research", "calibrated with other conversations")
- Provide a specific counter number with a proposed structure
- Ask an open question ("is there flexibility") rather than demanding

### Rule 3 — Use competing offers as leverage, gracefully.

Having another offer is the single most powerful negotiating asset. If you have one:

> "I want to be transparent — I'm also in late stages with [another company] and they've indicated an offer around $[X]k. Acme is my first choice and I'd love to close, but I'd need to see the numbers move closer to that to make it easy to say yes."

Rules:
- Never lie about a competing offer you don't have. If they ask for details you can't provide, it collapses instantly.
- Present it as information ("I want to be transparent"), not a threat ("match this or I'm out")
- Emphasize preference for the target company — you're not just picking based on money

If you don't have a competing offer, don't fake it. Instead lean on market data:

> "Based on Levels.fyi data for this level and location, the median TC is around $[X]k with the 75th percentile at $[Y]k. Given my background (10 years production experience, Master's, Google Cloud cert, six-month AI intensive), I feel this fits the upper end of that range."

### Rule 4 — Negotiate multiple things, not just one.

If they can't move base, ask for sign-on. If they can't move sign-on, ask for more equity. If they can't move equity, ask for a shorter cliff, an earlier performance review, or a level bump. Most companies have flexibility on *something* — find where.

**Sample multi-lever counter:**

> "I appreciate the offer. My counter would be one of these structures — happy to hear which is easier for you:
> 
> Option A: Bump base to $205k, keep sign-on and RSUs
> Option B: Keep base, bump sign-on to $65k
> Option C: Keep base and sign-on, bump initial RSU grant to $250k over 4 years
> Option D: Bump level from L4 to L5, adjust package to reflect
> 
> Any of these gets me comfortable saying yes. Which works on your end?"

This is friendly, specific, and shifts them from "no we can't move" to "let me see which lever is easiest."

### Rule 5 — Get the counter-response in writing.

After phone negotiation, always: "Thanks — can you send that updated offer via email?" Verbal offers evaporate; written offers commit.

### Rule 6 — Sign only after you've extracted everything.

Once you sign, negotiation is over. Even a "just one more thing" after signing is met with skepticism ("why didn't you raise this before?"). Make sure the final offer covers everything — start date, level, comp components, sign-on payment schedule, remote arrangement, PTO, any specific arrangement you need.

Common last-mile items:
- **Confirmed remote status.** Not "flexible on remote"; explicit "fully remote, no return-to-office."
- **Start date.** Enough buffer to finish current role professionally (typically 4 weeks notice + 1 week transition).
- **Existing PTO / paternity leave** — if you have plans, negotiate before signing.
- **Immigration / work-permit status** — if this applies to you internationally, get details in writing.

## Special Considerations for Remote Cross-Border Work

### US company hiring a US remote worker

Straightforward: W-2 employee, standard benefits, US taxes. Watch state-level tax implications if you move (e.g., Montana taxes vs California-based employer's obligations).

### US company hiring an EU worker (or vice versa)

Three common structures:
1. **Employer of Record (EOR)** — service like Deel, Remote.com, Oyster handles local employment. Employee gets local benefits + protections. Employer pays EOR fee (~10-15% of gross). Common for small-medium remote firms.
2. **Contractor arrangement** — you invoice as an independent contractor. Simpler for employer, more risk/complexity for you (self-employment taxes, no benefits, no protection).
3. **Local entity** — employer has a subsidiary in your country. Best benefits, hardest for small firms.

For Brian (US-based): most remote-friendly US employers hire you as a normal W-2 employee. Simple. Some European companies may hire you as a US contractor (get a good accountant).

### Tax treaties

If you split time between countries or work for a foreign employer:
- **US-EU tax treaties** cover most double-taxation risk, but you may still owe filings in both jurisdictions.
- **Foreign Earned Income Exclusion (FEIE)** — US citizens abroad can exclude ~$120k of foreign income. Doesn't help if you're US-based.
- **Get an accountant experienced with your specific setup.** ~$500-2000/year; pays for itself.

## The "Fair" Question

Should you negotiate?

**Yes.** Employers negotiate. Recruiters negotiate. Companies literally have "compensation bands" that presuppose most people don't push the top. Not negotiating leaves 10-30% on the table, forever (because raises are % of base).

Common concerns and responses:

- **"They'll rescind the offer if I push."** Almost never happens. Offers are made because they want you. Reasonable, respectful counters are expected and rarely cost anyone anything. The extreme cases (someone demanding 3× the offer with no market basis) can hurt; the normal 10-25% pushback almost never does.

- **"I don't want to seem greedy."** Being paid market rate for your work isn't greed. The employer already decided you're worth an offer. You're deciding on the exchange rate.

- **"I feel awkward talking about money."** Practice it out loud with a friend a few times. It becomes routine.

- **"I'm not sure I deserve more."** Your six-month AI curriculum + capstones + 10 years of production experience + Master's + GCP cert is a specific, real value. Deserving is not the question; the market rate is. If they offered, you're worth it.

## A Sample Negotiation Timeline

**Day 0** — Verbal offer on the phone from recruiter. You: "Thanks — sounds great. Can you send it via email?"

**Day 1** — Offer letter arrives via email. You reply: "Thanks — I'll review carefully and get back to you within 3 days."

**Day 1-2** — You research: Levels.fyi, network friends, comp target sheet. You draft a counter.

**Day 3** — Reply via email:
> Subject: Re: Offer Details — Senior AI Engineer
> 
> Hi [Recruiter],
> 
> Thanks again for the offer — I'm genuinely excited about [company] and the [role/team specifics].
> 
> I've done some research and want to share what I was hoping for based on market data and my background:
> 
> - Base: $205k (from $185k)
> - Sign-on: $50k (from $30k)
> - Equity: keep as is
> 
> Alternatively, I'd also be very open to:
> 
> - Base: keep at $185k
> - Sign-on: $75k
> - Equity: bump to $250k over 4 years
> 
> Either package makes this easy to say yes to. Is there flexibility on your end?
> 
> Looking forward to next steps,
> Brian

**Day 4-5** — Recruiter responds. Usually with a partial concession ("we can go to $195k base and $40k sign-on"). Sometimes with the ask granted. Sometimes with "let me check with the team."

**Day 6-8** — Final back-and-forth. You may push once more or accept.

**Day 9-10** — Accept in writing. Sign.

**Day 11+** — Give notice at current role.

## What You Should Never Do

- **Lie about competing offers** — one detail slip and the offer evaporates
- **Give an ultimatum you can't back up** — "match this or I walk" only works if you're actually willing to walk
- **Negotiate with the hiring manager** — go through the recruiter
- **Threaten** — never; only cooperate
- **Reveal your BATNA fully** — "if you don't match [other co]'s $290k I'll take theirs" reveals both your floor and your other offer's number
- **Accept the first offer without countering** — you're leaving 5-15% on the table statistically
- **Negotiate a role you don't actually want** — waste of everyone's time; you'll spend that energy joining a place you're already ambivalent about

## One Last Thing

Comp negotiation matters. It compounds over years. But it's not the most important thing about a role.

The rank ordering that predicts long-term happiness:
1. **What you'll actually work on** — is it interesting, is it high-agency, do you have influence?
2. **Who you'll work with** — smart peers, a manager who invests in you?
3. **Where the company is going** — growing, plateauing, declining?
4. **Compensation** — market rate at minimum; upside if the equity works out

Take the offer where the top three are yes and the fourth is at market. Don't take an offer where the fourth is 30% higher but the top three are ambiguous.

Good luck negotiating.
