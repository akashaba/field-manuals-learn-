Phase 10 · Lesson 07

RLHF — the reward model
and the leash.

SFT teaches the model to follow instructions. It does not teach the model which response is better. RLHF is how you encode human judgment into behavior — and it is what makes a modern assistant feel modern.

TypeBuild
LanguagePython + NumPy
PrereqLesson 06 (SFT)
Time~90 min

# Learning Objectives

  • Build a reward model that scores response quality from human preference pairs (chosen vs. rejected).
  • Implement the PPO training loop that optimizes a language-model policy against the reward model with a KL penalty.
  • Explain why RLHF needs three models (SFT, reward, policy) and how the KL constraint prevents reward hacking.
  • Evaluate the effect of RLHF by comparing response quality before and after preference optimization.

# The Problem

Ask a model “Explain quantum computing” and it might produce two answers that are both factually correct and grammatically sound — but a human would still pick one over the other every single time.

Response A. “Quantum computing uses qubits that can exist in superposition, meaning they can be 0, 1, or both simultaneously. This allows quantum computers to process certain calculations exponentially faster than classical computers. Key algorithms include Shor’s algorithm for factoring large numbers and Grover’s algorithm for searching unsorted databases.”

Response B. “Quantum computing is a type of computing that uses quantum mechanical phenomena. It was first proposed in the 1980s. Richard Feynman suggested that quantum systems could be simulated by quantum computers. The field has grown significantly since then. Many companies are now working on quantum computers. IBM, Google, and others have made progress. Quantum supremacy was claimed by Google in 2019.”

Both are correct. A is clearly better — more concise, better structured, more informative. And SFT cannot see the difference. SFT trains on “correct” responses; it has no mechanism to say “this one is better than that one.” If both A and B appeared in the SFT dataset, the model would learn from both equally.

RLHF solves this by training a reward model to predict which response a human prefers, and then using that reward signal to push the language model toward higher-quality outputs. InstructGPT — the precursor to ChatGPT — used RLHF to make GPT-3 dramatically more helpful. OpenAI’s evaluators preferred InstructGPT outputs over GPT-3 outputs 85% of the time, despite InstructGPT being 135× smaller (1.3 B vs. 175 B parameters). That is the entire pitch.

# The Three Stages

RLHF is a pipeline of three sequential stages, each building on the previous one. Every stage produces a model, and the last two produce the model artifacts you actually ship.

Stage 1 · SFT. Train a base model on instruction-response pairs (Lesson 06). Output: a model that follows instructions but does not know which response is better than another.

Stage 2 · Reward Model. Show annotators two responses to the same prompt and ask “which is better?” Train a model to predict these preferences. The reward model takes (prompt, response) as input and outputs a scalar score.

Stage 3 · PPO. Use the reward model to generate a training signal for the language model. The language model generates responses, the reward model scores them, and PPO updates the language model to produce higher-scoring responses. A KL divergence penalty prevents the language model from straying too far from the SFT checkpoint.

# The Reward Model

The reward model is a language model repurposed as a scorer. Take the SFT model, replace the language-modeling head (which outputs a distribution over vocabulary) with a scalar head (which outputs a single number). The architecture is identical up to the final layer.

Input: a prompt concatenated with a response. Output: one scalar. Training data: human preference triples (prompt, preferred_response, rejected_response).

The Bradley-Terry loss, visually

Why pairwise, not absolute? Humans are terrible at assigning absolute scores (“is this a 7.3 or a 7.5?”) but very good at relative comparisons. Bradley-Terry converts those relative comparisons into a consistent scalar scoring function. InstructGPT collected 33 K comparison pairs from 40 contractors, roughly 2 750 hours of human labor — for one reward model.

# PPO — the leash

PPO is a reinforcement-learning algorithm. In RLHF, the “environment” is the reward model, the “agent” is the language model, and the “action” is generating a token. The objective PPO maximizes is:

objective 
maximize:  E[ R(prompt, response) ]  −  β · KL( π_policy  ||  π_reference )

The first term pushes the model to generate high-reward responses. The second term — the leash — prevents the model from deviating too far from the SFT checkpoint. Without it, the model finds degenerate solutions: repeating “I’m so helpful and harmless!” because that phrase happens to score high on the reward model, or drifting into verbose, formal-sounding but empty responses that pattern-match to “quality.”

The clipped surrogate, in one paragraph

PPO’s stability trick is the clipped surrogate objective. The ratio between the new policy’s and the old policy’s probabilities is clipped to [1 − ε, 1 + ε], typically ε = 0.2. If a single response gets an unusually high reward, the unclipped ratio could be huge, causing the model to dramatically shift toward that one response. Clipping caps the update so a single lucky sample cannot rewrite the policy.

python 
ratio         = pi_new(action | state) / pi_old(action | state)
clipped_ratio = clip(ratio, 1 - epsilon, 1 + epsilon)
loss          = -min(ratio * advantage, clipped_ratio * advantage)

# advantage is how much better this response was than the baseline
advantage     = reward(prompt, response) - baseline

# Reward Hacking — the dark side

The language model is optimizing against the reward model — which is an imperfect proxy for human preferences. As the language model gets better at maximizing reward, it starts to exploit the reward model’s weaknesses. This is reward hacking, and it’s the reason the leash exists.

FailureWhat happensWhy
VerbosityResponses get longer and longerAnnotators preferred longer, more detailed responses, so the RM learned length ≈ quality.
SycophancyModel agrees with everything the user saysAnnotators preferred responses that agreed with the question’s premise.
HedgingModel refuses to commit to an answer“Complex topic with many perspectives…” rarely gets marked as wrong.
Format gamingBullet-list spamFormatted responses looked more “polished” to annotators.

Mitigations: a stronger KL penalty (prevents straying far enough to exploit weaknesses), training the RM on adversarial examples (patch known failure modes), and ensembling multiple RMs with different architectures (harder to hack them all simultaneously).

Step 2 · Reward-model architecture

The reward model reuses the transformer from the mini-GPT, but replaces the vocabulary-sized output head with a single scalar projection. Everything else — embeddings, blocks, layer-norm — is identical.

python 
class RewardModel:
    def __init__(self, vocab_size=256, embed_dim=128, num_heads=4,
                 num_layers=4, max_seq_len=128, ff_dim=512):
        self.embedding   = Embedding(vocab_size, embed_dim, max_seq_len)
        self.blocks      = [TransformerBlock(embed_dim, num_heads, ff_dim)
                            for _ in range(num_layers)]
        self.ln_f        = LayerNorm(embed_dim)
        self.reward_head = np.random.randn(embed_dim) * 0.02

    def forward(self, token_ids):
        seq_len = token_ids.shape[-1]
        mask    = np.triu(np.full((seq_len, seq_len), -1e9), k=1)

        x = self.embedding.forward(token_ids)
        for block in self.blocks:
            x = block.forward(x, mask)
        x = self.ln_f.forward(x)

        last_hidden = x[:, -1, :]        # last position — has seen everything
        reward      = last_hidden @ self.reward_head
        return reward

Why the last token? Because the causal attention mask means the last position has attended to every previous token — it has the most complete representation of the whole (prompt, response) sequence.

Step 3 · Bradley-Terry loss

python 
def sigmoid(x):
    return np.where(x >= 0,
                    1.0 / (1.0 + np.exp(-x)),
                    np.exp(x) / (1.0 + np.exp(x)))

def bradley_terry_loss(reward_preferred, reward_rejected):
    diff = reward_preferred - reward_rejected
    return -np.log(sigmoid(diff) + 1e-8)

def train_reward_model(rm, preference_data, num_epochs=10, lr=1e-4, max_seq_len=128):
    losses, accuracies = [], []
    for epoch in range(num_epochs):
        epoch_loss = 0.0
        correct    = 0
        for idx in np.random.permutation(len(preference_data)):
            pair = preference_data[idx]

            pref_ids = np.array(tokenize_for_reward(pair["prompt"], pair["preferred"])[:max_seq_len]).reshape(1, -1)
            rej_ids  = np.array(tokenize_for_reward(pair["prompt"], pair["rejected"])[:max_seq_len]).reshape(1, -1)

            r_pref = rm.forward(pref_ids)[0]
            r_rej  = rm.forward(rej_ids)[0]

            loss   = bradley_terry_loss(r_pref, r_rej)
            if r_pref > r_rej: correct += 1

            grad = sigmoid(r_pref - r_rej) - 1.0
            rm.reward_head -= lr * grad * rm.ln_f.forward(rm.embedding.forward(pref_ids))[:, -1, :].flatten()

            epoch_loss += loss
        losses.append(epoch_loss / len(preference_data))
        accuracies.append(correct / len(preference_data))
    return rm, losses, accuracies

Accuracy on preference pairs is the simplest metric: what fraction does the RM rank correctly? A random model scores 50%. A well-trained RM on clean data exceeds 70%. InstructGPT’s reward model achieved ~72% on held-out comparisons — which sounds low but is actually good; inter-annotator agreement was about 73%.

Step 4 · A minimal PPO loop

python 
def compute_kl_divergence(policy_logits, reference_logits):
    p = np.exp(policy_logits    - policy_logits.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True))
    p = p / p.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True);  p = np.clip(p, 1e-10, 1.0)
    r = np.exp(reference_logits - reference_logits.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True))
    r = r / r.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True);  r = np.clip(r, 1e-10, 1.0)
    return np.sum(p * np.log(p / r), axis=-1).mean()

def ppo_training(policy, reference, reward_model, prompts,
                 num_episodes=20, lr=1.5e-5, kl_coeff=0.02, max_seq_len=128):
    rewards_history, kl_history = [], []
    for episode in range(num_episodes):
        prompt_tokens  = [min(t, 252) for t in list(prompts[episode % len(prompts)].encode("utf-8"))]
        response       = generate_response(policy, prompt_tokens, max_new_tokens=20, temperature=0.8)
        response_ids   = np.array(response[:max_seq_len]).reshape(1, -1)

        reward         = reward_model.forward(response_ids)[0]
        kl             = compute_kl_divergence(policy.forward(response_ids),
                                               reference.forward(response_ids))
        total_reward   = reward - kl_coeff * kl

        # simplified gradient update — the real thing is the clipped surrogate
        for block in policy.blocks:
            step = lr * total_reward
            block.ffn.W1 += step * np.random.randn(*block.ffn.W1.shape) * 0.01
            block.ffn.W2 += step * np.random.randn(*block.ffn.W2.shape) * 0.01

        rewards_history.append(float(reward));  kl_history.append(float(kl))
    return policy, rewards_history, kl_history

Six lines carry the entire idea: sample a prompt, generate a response, score it, measure the KL against the frozen reference, subtract the leash cost, take a step. Everything else in production PPO — the value network, GAE advantages, importance sampling, minibatches, the actual clipped surrogate — is engineering around that core.

# Use It

After training, the policy model’s responses should score higher on the reward model than the original SFT model’s responses — while KL divergence against the reference stays bounded. If reward goes up and KL stays low, you’re winning. If reward goes up and KL explodes, you are reward-hacking; lower the temperature, raise β, or train a better reward model.

The 3 numbers to watch Reward trend — should climb, then plateau. KL trend — should climb slowly and settle. Response length — if it explodes without reward improvement, you’re hacking.

# Key Terms

TermWhat people sayWhat it actually means
RLHF“Training with human feedback”A three-stage pipeline (SFT, reward model, PPO) that optimizes language-model outputs using human preference signals.
Reward model“A model that scores responses”A transformer with a scalar output head, trained on pairwise human preferences with the Bradley-Terry loss.
Bradley-Terry“The comparison model”P(A ≻ B) = σ(score(A) − score(B)) — converts pairwise preferences into a consistent scoring function.
PPO“The RL algorithm”Proximal Policy Optimization — updates the policy to maximize reward while clipping the update magnitude to keep training stable.
KL penalty“The leash on the model”β · KL(π || π₀) subtracted from the reward — prevents the policy from diverging too far from the SFT checkpoint.
Reward hacking“Gaming the reward”When the policy finds degenerate high-reward outputs by exploiting weaknesses in the RM instead of genuinely improving.
Reference model“The frozen SFT checkpoint”A copy of the SFT model whose weights never change — the anchor for KL divergence computation.
